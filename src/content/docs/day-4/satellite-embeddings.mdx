## A new kind of image collection

Earth observation now produces far more images than anyone can process by hand, and turning them into maps usually needs a long chain of pre-processing (cloud masking, compositing, covariates) before any modelling. **Satellite embeddings** take a different route: a model learns, once, to summarise everything the satellites saw at each place over a year into a short list of numbers, and those numbers are published as an image collection ready to use.

The model is **AlphaEarth Foundations**, from Google DeepMind <Cite id="brown2025" />. The model was trained on data from millions of sites worldwide (the trainers' slide cites about 5 million locations, 10 million image sequences and 3 billion images, with land-cover maps among the training targets). It works like a *virtual satellite*: from sparse, irregular observations (optical, radar, lidar, climate and other sources), it produces a consistent, continuous summary of the surface for any place and year. The outputs are designed for practitioners, rather like an AI-made composite, and many different applications can be built on them.

## The Satellite Embedding dataset

```js
var embeddings = ee.ImageCollection('GOOGLE/SATELLITE_EMBEDDING/V1/ANNUAL');
```

<Image src={catalog} alt="Earth Engine Data Catalog page for the Satellite Embedding V1 dataset, with its availability, provider and code snippet." />

- **One image per year** per area (annual summaries, from 2017).
- **64 bands**, named `A00` to `A63`: the <Term id="embedding">embedding</Term> vector of each pixel. Together they form an *embedding field*.
- **10 m** pixels.
- Each pixel's vector has **unit length**: its 64 values, squared and summed, equal 1.

[Dataset page in the catalog](https://developers.google.com/earth-engine/datasets/catalog/GOOGLE_SATELLITE_EMBEDDING_V1_ANNUAL).

### What is embedded in an embedding?

For each 10 m pixel, the model looks at a window of about 1.28 km × 1.28 km around it and at the **whole calendar year** of observations from each sensor. It can use any information in that window, so the embedding encodes:

- what the pixel looks like across many sensors (spectral, radar, structure);
- how it **changes through the year** (seasonality, phenology), learned from image sequences rather than single dates;
- some of its **spatial context** (what surrounds it).

The 64 axes are learned, not named: no single axis means "forest" or "water". What matters is where a pixel's vector points: similar places point in similar directions.

<Callout type="why">
Why this matters: an embedding already contains seasonal and multi-sensor information that you would otherwise build by hand as composites and covariates. That is why embeddings often need fewer training samples for a good map.
</Callout>

## What can you do with embeddings?

| Task | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Similarity search** | find other places like a sample | built-up areas like central Thimphu |
| **Classification** | accurate maps with less training data | crop types, land cover |
| **Regression** | scale up measurements | above-ground biomass |
| **Change detection** | spot change and track processes | forest clearing |

## Under the hood: cosine similarity

Each pixel's embedding is a vector in 64-dimensional space. Two vectors are similar when they point in the same direction. Because the vectors have unit length, their **dot product** (multiply the 64 pairs of values and add them up) equals the cosine of the angle between them:

<Theory title="Dot product = cosine similarity">
For unit vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$, $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \sum_{i=0}^{63} a_i b_i = \cos\theta$. It runs from **1** (same direction: very similar) through **0** (at right angles: unrelated) to **−1** (opposite).
</Theory>

### Similarity search

Sample the embedding at one place, then compute its dot product with every other pixel: the result is a map of similarity, at 10 m, made on demand.

  <TabItem label="Python (geemap)">
```python
import ee
import geemap

ee.Initialize(project="<your-project>")  # your Earth Engine Cloud project ID

area = ee.Geometry.Point([89.64, 27.47]).buffer(8000)
emb2024 = (
    ee.ImageCollection("GOOGLE/SATELLITE_EMBEDDING/V1/ANNUAL")
    .filterDate("2024-01-01", "2025-01-01").filterBounds(area).mosaic()
)
built_up = ee.Geometry.Point([89.6419, 27.4661])
bands = emb2024.bandNames()
ref = emb2024.sample(region=built_up, scale=10).first()
ref_vector = ee.Image.constant(ref.toDictionary(bands).values(bands)).rename(bands)
similarity = emb2024.multiply(ref_vector).reduce(ee.Reducer.sum())

m = geemap.Map()
m.centerObject(area, 13)
m.addLayer(similarity.clip(area), {"min": 0, "max": 1, "palette": ["000004", "b73779", "fcfdbf"]}, "Similarity")
m
```

### Similarity in time: stability and change

Compare the same pixel's embedding in two years. High similarity means the place stayed the same; low similarity flags change:

```python
def get_year(year):
    return (
        ee.ImageCollection("GOOGLE/SATELLITE_EMBEDDING/V1/ANNUAL")
        .filterDate(f"{year}-01-01", f"{year + 1}-01-01").filterBounds(area).mosaic()
    )


stability = get_year(2017).multiply(get_year(2024)).reduce(ee.Reducer.sum())
m.addLayer(stability.clip(area), {"min": 0.5, "max": 1, "palette": ["d73027", "fee08b", "ffffff"]},
           "Similarity 2017 vs 2024 (low = change)")
```
  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

The model was trained to keep embeddings consistent through time, so similarity between years is a meaningful measure of stability. But "change" can mean a change of the **surface** (clearing, new buildings) or of the **environment** (a wet year against a dry one): check what you find.

## Classification and regression

Embeddings work well with simple and tree-based classifiers, because classes tend to be separable in the embedding space. You can use them in place of composites and covariates in an existing workflow, with **no pre-processing**, and scale up with Earth Engine. The next lesson, [Land cover and similarity with embeddings](/bhutan-hfld-training/day-4/embeddings-classification-similarity/), classifies Bhutan's land cover from them.

Expect good results with fewer training samples, and excellent results with large, high-quality ones. Embeddings also handle rare classes better and support continuous variables (regression), and they don't need wall-to-wall training labels.

## Good practices

- **Quality over quantity.** A few hundred well-placed, correct labels beat thousands of noisy ones.
- **Match labels to years.** Use the embedding of the year your label describes.
- **"Annualise" labels.** Labels should describe the whole year, as the embedding does, not one day.
- **Points are easier than polygons** for mapping; to classify polygons or patches, average the embeddings inside them.
- **Remember the space is learned and unit-length.** Use dot products (cosine similarity) to compare; don't read single axes as physical quantities.

Explore the 64-dimensional space and cosine similarity in the [embedding similarity visualiser](/bhutan-hfld-training/labs/embedding-similarity/).

<Callout type="bhutan">
Bhutan context: cloud cover limits optical composites in the monsoon; embeddings are built from many sensors, including radar, over the whole year, so they can help where Sentinel-2 alone struggles. Check them against your own maps before relying on them for reporting, and document which year's embeddings you used.
</Callout>

## Key functions

| Function | What it does |
|---|---|
| `ee.ImageCollection('GOOGLE/SATELLITE_EMBEDDING/V1/ANNUAL')` | The annual embedding images |
| `image.sample({region, scale}).first()` | Reads the 64 values at a point |
| `feature.toDictionary(bands).values(bands)` | Turns them into a list in band order |
| `ee.Image.constant(list)` | Makes a constant image from that vector |
| `image.multiply(other).reduce(ee.Reducer.sum())` | Dot product, pixel by pixel = cosine similarity |

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing years**: labels from 2020 with 2024 embeddings.
- **Interpreting one band** (`A16`) as a physical variable.
- **Using Euclidean distances on rescaled values.** Keep the unit vectors and use dot products.
- **Reading all low similarity as land-cover change**; some is seasonal or climatic.
