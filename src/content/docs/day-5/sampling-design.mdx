## Why not use the map as it is?

A land-cover map is a model, and models make errors:

- **Classification errors.** Some pixels are in the wrong class.
- **No sign of over- or under-estimation.** Counting pixels doesn't tell you whether the map has too much or too little of a class.
- **No uncertainty.** A pixel count has no confidence interval.

For example, a plot the map labels **deforestation** may turn out, on high-resolution imagery, to be a **forest edge**:

<Image src={ceoMap} alt="Collect Earth Online plot over the stratification map, with the class legend." />

<Image src={ceoImagery} alt="The same Collect Earth Online plot over high-resolution imagery." />

So we take a **sample**: points whose true class is checked against better data (high-resolution imagery or field visits). The sample lets us:

1. estimate the area of each class without bias;
2. assess the map's accuracy;
3. quantify the uncertainty of the estimates <Cite id="olofsson2014" />.

## Stratification

**Strata** divide the area into parts that are sampled separately. In practice, the strata are the **map classes** (or groups of them), because:

- **detection accuracy differs** between land-cover types, so some classes need more samples than others;
- **accuracy requirements differ**: for example, a minimum accuracy may be required for forest;
- **rare classes**, such as deforestation, would hardly be sampled at all without their own stratum (see [Change detection and loss strata](/lulc-mapping-training/day-3/change-strata/)).

Methodologies can also require specific stratification, for example by **deforestation risk** and **forest type**.

<Image src={frame} alt="Forest and non-forest map of Bhutan used as the sampling frame." />

<Theory title="Why strata make estimates more precise" more="/explanation/area-estimation/">
Within each stratum the samples are more alike than across the whole map, so each stratum's estimate has a smaller variance. Weighting each stratum's results by its share of the mapped area, $W_h = A_h/A$, combines them into an unbiased estimate for the whole area. Any allocation of samples to strata stays unbiased as long as these weights are used.
</Theory>

## Probability sampling designs

Every design below is a **probability sample**: each unit (pixel or plot) has a known chance of selection, which is what makes unbiased estimation possible.

| Design | How | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Simple random** | every sampling unit (pixel) has an equal chance of being selected | the most potential sampling units, so the sample is easy to enlarge | points can be hard to reach in the field |
| **Systematic** | samples at fixed distances and directions from each other (a grid) | easy to locate in the field | fewer potential units; spatial patterns that line up with the grid (terrain, field layouts) must be identified and stratified |
| **Cluster** | a cluster of samples around each selected unit; combinable with random or systematic selection | more confident interpretation, since nearby samples help | samples within a cluster are correlated; time-consuming |

With a **stratified** design, any of these is applied within each stratum: for example, stratified random sampling draws a simple random sample in each map class.

<Image src={randomPts} alt="Stratified sample points shown over a forest map of part of Bhutan." />

<Image src={systematicPts} alt="Close-up of the stratified map with sample points marked in white." />

<Image src={clusters} alt="Clustered sample plots, each a 3 by 3 grid of points, placed over a classified map." />

<Callout type="why">
Why this matters: the design determines which formulas you use to estimate areas and their uncertainty. Stratified random sampling, used in this course, has simple, well-established estimators.
</Callout>

## Sample size

Two basic ideas:

- **Area proportions determine how samples are allocated** between strata;
- **expected accuracy determines the total sample size.**

It is a balance between minimum accuracy requirements and the level of effort (LOE).

| Larger sample | Smaller sample |
|---|---|
| estimated accuracy closer to the actual accuracy | less effort, less time, less quality control |

Keep in mind:

- **A larger sample doesn't make the map more accurate**; it makes the *estimate* of accuracy more precise.
- **Stratification quality is critical.** Strata should be modular (easy to divide) and represent reality.
- **Increase the sample strategically** if your first assumptions about accuracy turn out wrong.

[OpenMRV](https://openmrv.org/)'s module "Sampling design for estimation of area and map accuracy" covers these choices in detail. The exercise for this step is [Determining sample size](/lulc-mapping-training/day-5/sample-size/), with the [sample size calculator lab](/lulc-mapping-training/labs/sample-size-calculator/).

## Earth Engine functions for sampling

| Function | Use |
|---|---|
| `image.reduceRegion()` | computes a statistic of a raster within a geometry: here, the area of each stratum |
| `ee.Reducer` | the statistic for `reduceRegion` (for example a grouped sum) |
| `ee.Image.pixelArea()` | the area of each pixel in m²; divide by 10,000 for hectares |
| `image.updateMask()` | removes areas from the sampling frame |
| `image.stratifiedSample()` | takes a list of class values and a list of sample sizes, and places points at the centres of randomly selected pixels of each class. Ask for more than you need, then trim each stratum later |
| `geometry.buffer()` | turns a point into a circle: clustered samples, or checking for overlap |
| `geometry.bounds()` | turns a circle into a square: square sampling plots |
| `collection.iterate()` | runs a function cumulatively: for example checking overlaps between samples; computationally heavy, so run it as a separate step |
| `collection.limit()` | trims a collection to a given size: the sample of each stratum |

[Stratified sampling in GEE](/lulc-mapping-training/day-5/stratified-sampling-gee/) uses these in practice.

Bhutan context: the course's Day 5 design stratifies by forest (split into three strata), non-forest and deforestation, and allocates samples so the small deforestation stratum is well covered. Steep terrain makes field visits hard, so reference data come mostly from high-resolution imagery in Collect Earth Online.
</Callout>

## Common mistakes

- **Reporting pixel counts** as areas.
- **Using training data as reference data.** Reference samples must come from a probability sample, independent of training.
- **A systematic grid that lines up with landscape patterns** (terraces, ridges) without stratifying them.
- **Forgetting the weights**: averaging all samples equally when strata were sampled at different rates.

## Practice

<Challenge level="complete" time={5} hints={["Think about field access, interpretation effort and correlation."]}>
For a national reference sample for Bhutan, interpreted mostly from imagery, which design would you choose: simple random, systematic or clustered (each stratified by map class)? Give one reason for and one against.

<Fragment slot="solution">
A reasonable choice is **stratified simple random**: imagery interpretation doesn't need field access, so the disadvantage of random points (hard to reach) hardly matters, and the estimators are simple. Against: random points can fall in hard-to-interpret places (cloud, shadow). Clustered plots would make interpretation more confident but correlate the samples and add effort; systematic grids risk lining up with terraces and ridges.
</Fragment>
