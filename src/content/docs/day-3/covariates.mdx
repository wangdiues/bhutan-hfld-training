In machine learning for maps, <Term id="covariate">covariates</Term> are variables derived from the data that describe the land surface. They are the inputs (features) to classifiers such as Random Forest, Gradient Boosted Trees or Support Vector Machines. Raw bands alone often can't separate similar classes; covariates make the differences explicit.

For HFLD mapping, covariates capture:

| Property | Examples |
|---|---|
| vegetation condition | NDVI, EVI, SAVI, Tasseled Cap greenness |
| water presence and dynamics | NDWI, MNDWI, JRC surface water |
| built-up areas and soil | NDBI, IBI, Tasseled Cap brightness |
| terrain | elevation, slope, aspect, eastness, northness |
| seasonality | every spectral covariate for both Jan–Apr and Oct–Dec |

Better covariates mean better predictions: they help distinguish similar classes (forest and crops, water and shadow), each describes a different property, and the seasonal pairs capture changes over the year, which matter for crops, deciduous forest and water.

**Inputs:** the Landsat 8/9 (or Sentinel-2) seasonal composite from Day 2, the [SRTM DEM](https://developers.google.com/earth-engine/datasets/catalog/USGS_SRTMGL1_003), and the [JRC Global Surface Water](https://developers.google.com/earth-engine/datasets/catalog/JRC_GSW1_4_GlobalSurfaceWater) dataset.

What the function computes:

| Group | Covariates | What they describe |
|---|---|---|
| Normalised differences | NDVI, NDWI, NBR, NDSI, and four more band pairs (red–SWIR1, NIR–SWIR1, NIR–SWIR2, green–SWIR1) | vegetation, water, burn scars and moisture, snow |
| Ratios | SWIR1 / NIR, red / SWIR1 | moisture and soil versus vegetation |
| Vegetation | EVI, SAVI (L = 0.5) | vegetation in dense canopy (EVI) and where soil shows (SAVI) |
| Built-up | NDBI, MNDWI, IBI (index-based built-up index, Xu 2008) | settlements and bare surfaces versus vegetation and water |
| Tasseled Cap | brightness (TCB), greenness (TCG), wetness (TCW), and components 4–6 | soil and brightness, green vegetation, moisture |
| Tasseled Cap angles and distances | angles and distances in the brightness–greenness, greenness–wetness and brightness–wetness planes | forest structure and disturbance |

<Callout type="mistake" title="Corrected: scale the composite first">
The Day 2 composite stores reflectance × 10,000 as Int16. The training script used those values directly, which gives wrong EVI, SAVI and IBI (their constants assume 0–1 reflectance) and Tasseled Cap values 10,000 times too large. The course script divides each band by 10,000 first. The normalised differences and ratios are not affected.
</Callout>

<Theory title="Tasseled Cap">
The Tasseled Cap transformation rotates the six reflective bands into new axes with physical meaning: **brightness** (overall reflectance, mostly soil and bare ground), **greenness** (NIR against visible: green vegetation) and **wetness** (visible and NIR against SWIR: moisture in soil and canopy). The coefficients here are those commonly used for Landsat 8/9 OLI. Angles and distances between pairs of components summarise where a pixel sits in that space, which helps separate forest from shrubs, crops and disturbed land.
</Theory>

The function also builds a `tc` image with a matrix of coefficients and then, as its comment says, computes the same components explicitly because the matrix approach is awkward in Earth Engine. The unused `tc` variable does no harm: Earth Engine only computes what is used.

## Part 4: Terrain covariates

Terrain shapes where vegetation grows, especially in Bhutan. From SRTM, compute elevation, slope and aspect. Aspect is an angle (0° and 360° are both north), so it is also turned into **eastness** (sine of aspect) and **northness** (cosine of aspect), which a classifier can use as smooth values from −1 to 1.

<Tabs>

```python
import math

import ee

ee.Initialize(project="<your-project>")  # your Earth Engine Cloud project ID

dem = ee.Image("USGS/SRTMGL1_003").rename("elevation")
terrain = ee.Terrain.products(dem)
aspect_rad = terrain.select("aspect").multiply(math.pi / 180)
terrain_cov = ee.Image.cat([
    dem,
    terrain.select("slope"),
    terrain.select("aspect"),
    aspect_rad.sin().rename("eastness"),
    aspect_rad.cos().rename("northness"),
])
print(terrain_cov.bandNames().getInfo())
```

## Key functions

| Function | What it does |
|---|---|
| `ee.Image.cat([...])` | Stacks images into one multi-band image |
| `a.subtract(b).divide(a.add(b))` | Normalised difference |
| `x.atan2(y)`, `x.pow(2).add(y.pow(2)).sqrt()` | Angle and distance between two components |
| `ee.Terrain.products(dem)` | Slope, aspect and hillshade from a DEM |
| `image.sampleRegions({collection, properties, scale})` | Reads band values at points |
| `ee.Classifier.smileRandomForest({...}).train({...})` | Trains a Random Forest |

## Common mistakes

- **Using scaled integers in formulas with constants.** Divide by 10,000 first (see above).
- **Raw aspect as a covariate only.** 359° and 1° look far apart; add eastness and northness.
- **Too many near-identical covariates.** Dozens of strongly correlated indices add little and slow training; check variable importance (next lesson).
- **Mixing seasons by name.** Keep the `_jan_apr` and `_oct_dec` suffixes on everything.
- **Training points without a numeric `class` property.** `train()` needs it.

<Callout type="bhutan">
Bhutan context: terrain covariates matter more here than in flat countries. Elevation separates subtropical, temperate and alpine vegetation; northness separates moist north-facing forest from drier south-facing slopes; and the Oct–Dec season shows deciduous forest and harvested fields differently from Jan–Apr.
</Callout>
